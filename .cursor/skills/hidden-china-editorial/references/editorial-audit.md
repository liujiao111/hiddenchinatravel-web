# Hidden China Editorial Audit Reference

Use this reference for a review or minimal edit. Flag a pattern only when it reduces clarity, trust, usefulness, or author voice. Do not rewrite clean prose merely because it is polished.

## 1. Fact and source risk

Flag:

- exact prices, schedules, ticket rules, operating hours, regulations, and availability without a current primary source;
- an opinion written as universal fact (“Lijiang is less commercial than Dali”);
- claims of personal experience not supplied by Joy;
- invented shop, driver, hotel, route, or food recommendations;
- an assertion that a foreign visitor can book, enter, or use a service without caveats around passports, local apps, payment, or current rules;
- a named, bookable business (hotel, tour, ticketed activity) that has not had its guest-eligibility, age, or check-in rules checked against its own listing page — a source-material draft praising a place says nothing about whether this site's foreign-passport readers can actually use it.

Fix:

- verify with the official operator, government, railway, airline, attraction, or platform;
- remove the number/detail when it does not change the answer;
- make the author basis explicit: “In my experience…” or “I would choose…”;
- say what to check before the trip.

## 2. The “could fit anywhere” test

For each paragraph, ask: could this appear unchanged in a guide to Guilin, Chiang Mai, or Lisbon?

If yes, add one of:

- a real place, system, route choice, or traveler mistake;
- a specific trade-off;
- Joy’s supported local judgment;
- a useful instruction;
- or delete it.

Do not add decorative specificity that the author cannot stand behind.

## 3. Promotional and tourism-board language

Flag clusters, not a single unavoidable word:

- amazing, magical, ultimate, unforgettable, breathtaking, vibrant;
- hidden gem (unless it is the page’s search intent and the place is honestly less visited);
- must-see, must-visit, iconic, world-class;
- “nestled,” “boasts,” “showcases,” “a testament to,” “in the heart of”;
- “perfect for everyone,” “there is something for everyone.”

Fix with a usable observation:

- “Stay here if you want to walk to dinner.”
- “The old town is busy on holidays; use it for convenience, then leave the main streets for quieter food.”
- “This cable-car day only works if the weather is clear.”

## 4. Formulaic reasoning and structure

Flag:

- “not just X, but Y” used as a rhetorical flourish;
- forced triples and mirror-image bullet lists;
- a conclusion that restates the introduction;
- headings created only to hit a target count;
- identical paragraph length or cadence across the entire article;
- long preambles before the answer;
- “In conclusion,” “Ultimately,” “It is important to note,” or “When it comes to.”

Fix:

- state the recommendation once;
- keep the useful list and remove the decorative one;
- vary length because the information varies, not to simulate randomness;
- end after the reader’s next action is clear.

## 5. Language-level AI tells

Flag when dense or repeated:

- `delve`, `navigate` used metaphorically, `leverage`, `utilize`, `facilitate`;
- `landscape` when “situation,” “route,” or a concrete noun is meant;
- `seamless`, `robust`, `comprehensive`, `crucial`, `key`, `valuable`, `transformative`;
- noun-heavy abstractions such as “the importance of planning considerations”;
- empty intensifiers: `really`, `very`, `incredibly`, `particularly` without a measurable reason;
- vague attribution: “many people say,” “travelers often find,” “it is widely considered.”

Preferred repairs:

- `use`, not `utilize`;
- `is`, not `serves as`;
- `has`, not `boasts`;
- name the actual person, place, condition, or source—or delete the attribution.

## 6. Human voice, without fake voice

Keep genuine first-person judgment:

- “I recommend three nights.”
- “I would not buy silver in the scenic-area shops.”
- “For a first visit, I would stay in the old town if you are undecided.”

Do not manufacture:

- anecdotes;
- fake uncertainty;
- slang or fragments;
- emotional reactions the author did not provide;
- claims that “locals always…” or “no one tells you…”.

The desired result is not casual. It is a named person making an honest, useful recommendation.

## 7. Destination-guide checks

Each destination guide should answer:

1. Who is this place for?
2. How long is enough?
3. Where should a first-time independent visitor base themselves?
4. What should they skip, shorten, or not over-plan?
5. What China-specific system could make the trip fail if ignored?
6. What is the correct next page or action?

Do not answer every specialist question at full length in a pillar. Give a decision-level answer, then link to a published specialist page when one exists.

## 8. Conversion checks

Before adding a CTA, identify the reader’s state:

- exploring → a relevant guide or the Survival Kit;
- deciding city order, nights, pace, or base → itinerary planner;
- ready to execute a booking → an appropriate disclosed affiliate link.

Reject:

- planner CTAs after every H2;
- a generic “Plan my China trip” where a Dali/Yunnan-specific CTA is needed;
- pricing copied from another trip-length tier;
- affiliate links that decide the editorial recommendation;
- any CTA that implies a guided tour, promised booking inventory, or on-the-ground accompaniment.

## 9. Fidelity pass after edits

Check that editing did not change:

- place names, Chinese names, dates, figures, prices, and quotes;
- the direction or destination of an internal or affiliate link;
- an explicitly supplied author judgment;
- the distinction between verified fact and personal recommendation;
- CTA service scope, fee policy, or route price tier.

Return a short summary with:

- changes made;
- factual items needing confirmation;
- intentionally retained author voice;
- any conversion / SEO issue that requires a product or engineering change rather than copy editing.
